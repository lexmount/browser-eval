The Nutrition 2025 meeting hosted annually by the American Society for Nutrition (ASN) brought together leading experts, thought leaders, and clinicians to Orlando to showcase the latest in nutrition science and practice. I've compiled 5 trends that stood out to me while walking the expo hall and sitting in sessions over 4 days, with relevant takeaways for nutrition communications professionals.

1. From Lab to Literature, Artificial Intelligence Has Strong Showing

Artificial intelligence (AI) made a strong showing at Nutrition 2025 as researchers demonstrated its growing role in nutrition science – from lab to literature. One session from the Student Interest Group of ASN explored how AI is reshaping research and publishing, with experts from academia and industry sharing how they use AI tools to accelerate and improve data analysis and literature reviews (this was also prevalent in the poster sessions as well). There is a need to understand now to use AI ethically as well – knowing when to rely on what specific algorithms or models and how to defend AI-assisted findings during the peer review process given the stigma that may come with using AI currently. Beyond scientific publishing, AI is also driving discovery of novel nutrients and bioactive compounds.

In a forward-looking symposium session, scientists showed how AI can be used to explore bioactive plant compounds and essentially turn them into clinically backed ingredients – for example, identifying new phytochemicals that improve gut health. This fusion of AI and nutrition science is enabling breakthroughs and discovery at a pace previously “impossible” by human analysis alone.

Takeaway for Communicators: If your brand or organization uses AI insights or findings externally, be prepared to explain AI-driven findings in simple terms. As AI becomes integral to nutrition research, communicators should highlight the benefits (faster insights, new discoveries) while clarifying the limitations and ethical use of these tools. Consider emphasizing transparency and credibility when translating AI-assisted research to stakeholders to make clear that “smart tech” still relies on sound science.

2. Science StorEATelling

This year’s conference underscored the value of culinary experiences in bringing nutrition science to life. Rather than “dry” lectures alone, some lunchtime programs invited attendees to immerse themselves in the science through food – literally and figuratively consuming the science. For example, a sponsored session on cultural food traditions featured a chef alongside researchers to demonstrate how traditional flavors and dishes can align with dietary guidelines (#client). Another session on “eating with and for our eyes” included a custom “tasting plate” lunch menu with foods purported to benefit eye health (#client). Such interactive learning experiences allowed participants not just to hear about nutrition evidence, but to taste and see it in action.

This trend toward culinary medicine – integrating dietary guidance with cooking demonstrations and tastings – reflects an effort to make nutrition knowledge tangible. Other presentations critically evaluated how hands-on culinary interventions (like teaching patients to cook healthy meals) can improve health outcomes and discussed how to translate these immersive approaches to broader community settings. The big idea: engaging the senses can reinforce the science.

Takeaway for Communicators: Nutrition communicators should consider leveraging culinary experiences at professional meetings or conferences to engage their audiences. Whether it’s hosting cooking demos, sharing recipes that illustrate research findings, or using vivid food imagery, bringing “taste” and “touch” to science can make your message more relatable. When people can smell, see, or taste what they’re learning, the science sticks. Don’t forget the important role of visuals in event production as well, such as having color cues that align with the message to enhance the learning experience.

3. GLP-1 Weight-Loss Drugs Steal the Spotlight With Nutrition Alongside

It’s no surprise that GLP-1 agonist drugs (like semaglutide and tirzepatide) were everywhere at Nutrition 2025 – these anti-obesity medications are transforming weight management and were a hot topic in hallways and sessions alike. Researchers presented cutting-edge findings on how these drugs “modulate appetite-regulating circuits and metabolic pathways,” offering unprecedented weight loss outcomes. But a clear theme was that medication alone isn’t a magic bullet. Across multiple talks, experts emphasized the need for comprehensive strategies combining pharmacology with lifestyle changes including nutrition interventions or hydration needs (see here and here, #client).

In other words, nutrition and behavior still matter – perhaps more than ever – for patients on GLP-1 therapy. In fact, just days before Nutrition 2025, the American College of Lifestyle Medicine (ACLM), ASN, the Obesity Medicine Association (OMA), and The Obesity Society (TOS) jointly released a clinical advisory titled “Nutritional Priorities to Support GLP-1 Therapy for Obesity,” which highlighted that evidence-based diet and lifestyle interventions enhance GLP-1 treatment outcomes, improving patient adherence and overall health. Discussions ranged from ensuring patients on these appetite-suppressing drugs get enough protein and micronutrients, to messaging that explored the notion “you can eat anything” on GLP-1s.

Takeaway for Communicators: Understanding how your organization or brand’s target audience interacts with GLP-1 weight-loss drugs is crucial. Whether your audience consists of consumers currently using the drugs or healthcare professionals recommending them, messaging should acknowledge the nuanced role of these medications and how your product or offering fits in. Focus on how your brand can serve as a valuable partner in the journey and any practical guidance – such as the importance of nutrient-dense foods and balanced meals, even with reduced appetite.

4. The Ultra-Processed Foods Debate Isn’t Going Anywhere

Sessions on ultra-processed foods (UPFs) were abundant at Nutrition 2025. A dedicated session on UPFs was widely attended, where researchers presented new data from the RESTRUCTURE trial, which is probing how the sensory properties of foods (like texture and eating speed) influence calorie intake and satiety. The data presented at the meeting suggest that over a 14-day period, participants consuming a UPF diet with textures that reduced their eating rate, had an average energy intake reduction of 369 kcal per day, compared to when they followed a 14-day UPF diet with textures that promoted a faster eating rate. In other words, researchers believe the texture of food can affect how quickly we eat and, in turn, how many calories we consume. Consensus at the conference was that UPFs will remain a central research and communication topic. If anything, the conversation, at least on the scientific side of things, is evolving – moving beyond demonization towards nuanced questions about the “why.”

Takeaway for Communicators: The ultra-processed foods (UPF) conversation will continue and evolve, and as nutrition communicators, it’s crucial to actively monitor the ongoing dialogue. There are evolving perspectives on UPFs, especially regarding their definition and their impact on health. It’s important for communicators to stay informed about the latest research, like the sensory impact of food texture on calorie intake, as seen in the RESTRUCTURE trial, and other metabolic findings related to UPFs.

5. Protein Science Continues to Dominate

If one nutrient reigned supreme at Nutrition 2025, it was protein. From muscle health to metabolic advantages, protein was at the center of many presentations, reflecting its ongoing star status in nutrition science. A full-day special “Dietary Protein for Global Human Health” workshop brought together leading experts to share the latest research on protein quality and requirements across populations. A key focus was on protein quality – not just quantity – and how better metrics like DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score) can inform global nutrition strategies.

Experts discussed using high-quality proteins to combat malnutrition and other health issues in vulnerable groups (e.g. children, the elderly) and even presented ideas for integrating protein quality considerations into dietary guidelines. Beyond this, numerous studies and sessions presented at the conference examined protein’s role in satiety and weight management – reinforcing findings that higher-protein diets can aid in appetite control and body composition.

Takeaway for Communicators: Leverage the public’s interest in protein – but do so responsibly. The continued spotlight on protein means audiences are receptive to messages about getting enough protein and choosing high-quality sources. Communicators should translate the latest science (e.g. explain what “protein quality” means and why not all proteins are equal) into consumer-friendly advice.
